Before you start

This guide assumes you know your rank and category and the overall CAP sequence. For registration through reporting, see the companion guide "Complete CAP Process Walkthrough". Rules here are described as they applied in the 2026-27 cycle; check this year's notices before you fill your form.

1. Why the option form decides more than your score

Two students with the same rank can end up at very different colleges because one filled a better option form. In each round, the allotment gives you the highest college + branch on your list that your merit position reaches. A seat that is not on your list can never be allotted to you, however strong your rank.

Key principle

Every eligible choice you leave off is a seat you have already given up. Listing a realistic choice lower down costs you nothing, with one exception: auto-freeze, covered next, means your top few choices deserve extra thought.

2. The rules that shape your list

A fresh option form each round

In the 2026 cycle, the vacant seats were published before each round and a new option-form window opened (Round II: 7 to 9 August; Round III: 20 to 22 August; Round IV: 30 August to 1 September). Your list is not fixed after Round I: you can and should rebuild it from that round's vacancies. To be considered for a better seat in a round, submit that round's form within its window.

Freeze, Float and Slide

OptionWhat happensWhen to choose it
FreezeYou keep this seat and leave the later rounds.You are sure you want this college and branch as your final seat.
Float (Not Freeze / betterment)You keep this seat as a safety net and stay eligible for a higher choice. A better allotment replaces it; otherwise you keep it.You would move for a better college or branch if one comes up.
Slide (where offered)Like Float, but only for a better branch at the same institute.You like the college and only want a better branch there. Check whether this year's notice offers it.

Whichever you choose, you still accept the seat and report within that round's window. In 2026 the seat acceptance fee was ₹1,000, paid once at your first acceptance.

Auto-freeze changes how you order your top choices

In the 2026 cycle a seat was frozen automatically when it matched one of your top preferences for that round: your 1st choice in Round I, one of your first 3 in Round II, or one of your first 6 in Round III. An auto-frozen seat is final: you pay the fee, report, and take no part in later rounds.

So the top of each round's list is not just "most wanted". It must be seats you would genuinely be happy to end with. A long-shot dream at #1 is fine if you would take it; a college you are only lukewarm about is not.

3. The three-zone method: Dream, Target, Safe

Sort every college + branch you are considering by comparing your rank with its most recent closing rank for your category and seat type. Compare ranks, not percentiles: percentiles are normalised and bunch together near the top, while the closing rank is the exact merit position of the last student admitted. A smaller rank number is better.

ZoneYour rank vs the closing rankPurposeShare of list
DreamYour rank number is somewhat larger than the closing rank: you would have needed a little luck.Ambitious picks; cutoffs move every year and seats free up in later rounds.15–25%
TargetClose to the closing rank, on either side.Your most likely outcome; give it the most entries and the most careful ordering.45–55%
SafeYour rank number is clearly smaller than the closing rank.Insurance, so you never end a round with no seat.20–30%

CET Hub's College Predictor uses the same idea with a band that scales with your rank (at rank 10,000, Dream covers seats that closed between ranks 5,000 and 7,500, Target 7,500 to 11,500 and Safe 11,500 to 15,000). The shares above are a starting point, not a rule. The rule that does not bend: every list needs a Safe zone, because last year's cutoff is a guide, not a promise.

Rohan, OPEN category, state-level seats, rank about 12,000. He wants Computer Engineering or IT, would accept E&TC as a backup, and is open to Pune and Mumbai. Within each zone the order is Rohan's real preference, not the easiest option first, and the list mixes colleges because branch matters to him as much as the institute.

The auto-freeze check

Before submitting, Rohan asks of his top choice: "If I get this in Round I, am I happy for it to be final?" College A Computer Engineering: yes. If his #1 had been a college he only half wanted, he would move it down, because a Round I allotment of the 1st choice was frozen automatically in 2026.

Why ten rows, not three

A list of three "sure things" guarantees a seat, probably worse than Rohan could have earned. In real CAP most students list far more than ten; the form allows many choices, and 20 to 30 well-ordered rows is common.

5. Ordering: college-first or branch-first?

Decide deliberately what you are optimising for; the two approaches give very different lists.

ApproachSuits you whenHow to order
College-firstCampus, location and placements matter most; you are flexible on branch.List a college's best-fit branch, then its next branch, before moving to the next college.
Branch-firstYou have a clear career direction, such as computer science, and would take it at a slightly less known college.List your target branch across every eligible college before other branches anywhere.
Hybrid (most common)You have 2 or 3 acceptable branches and a shortlist of acceptable colleges.Rank each college + branch pair by genuine preference, as in the example.

Whichever you choose, apply it consistently. A list that switches logic halfway is a common source of regret.

6. Round-by-round strategy

  • Round I: submit your fullest, most carefully zoned list. Most seats are still open. Your 1st choice must be one you would keep as final.
  • After any allotment: accept and report within the window, then choose Freeze or Float. Floating keeps your seat as a safety net.
  • Rounds II and III: read the published vacancies and rebuild your list for that round's option form. Remember auto-freeze widens: your first 3 choices in Round II and first 6 in Round III were frozen automatically in 2026.
  • Round IV: the final CAP round. The seat you hold afterwards is final.
  • After CAP: vacant seats go to institute-level admission, which has its own process and deadlines.

7. Ten mistakes that cost a better seat

  1. Listing too few choices. Five rows limit what the allotment can give you; extra realistic rows cost nothing.
  2. Only "sure" colleges. Guarantees a seat, usually a worse one than your rank could earn.
  3. No Safe zone. An all-ambitious list risks ending a round with no seat.
  4. A lukewarm college in your top slots. With auto-freeze, it can become your final seat.
  5. Skipping a later round's option form. In 2026 each round had its own window; missing it meant no shot at a better seat that round.
  6. Freezing out of impatience. Floating keeps your seat and may upgrade it.
  7. Treating a college as one cutoff. Computer and Civil at the same college can differ by thousands of ranks.
  8. Using last year's numbers as fact. Cutoffs move with exam difficulty, applicants and seat changes.
  9. Checking the wrong cutoff row. Match your category, gender (L codes are ladies-only seats) and seat type: State Level, Home University or Other Than Home University.
  10. Missing acceptance or reporting. An allotted seat can be lost by missing that round's window.

8. Quick checklist

  • I compared my rank with closing ranks for my exact category, gender and seat type.
  • My list has Dream, Target and Safe entries, with a solid Safe zone.
  • Every choice in my top slots is a seat I would be happy to keep as final.
  • I chose college-first, branch-first or hybrid deliberately and applied it consistently.
  • I know each round's option-form window and will rebuild my list from that round's vacancies.
  • I know the acceptance and reporting deadline for every round.
  • I checked this year's notices on cethub.in/updates, not just last year's rules.
